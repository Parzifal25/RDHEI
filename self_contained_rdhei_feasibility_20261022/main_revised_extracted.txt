Abstract
In prediction-based reversible data hiding in encrypted images (RDHEI), ca-
pacity is governed by how prediction errors are distributed, which makes the
predictor a primary design variable. We study a compact convolutional neu-
ral network (CNN) pixel predictor (46,881 parameters) inside a label-map
most-signicant-bit (MSB) replacement pipeline in which the content owner
embeds into the plaintext and encrypts the marked image with AES-CTR.
Holding the embedding and recovery algebra xed, we compare the CNN
with MED, GAP and CALIC and with four controlled CNN variants. On
six standard grayscale benchmark images the CNN reaches a mean gross ca-
pacity of 5.190 bpp (GAP 5.027) and a mean prediction MAE of 4.46 (GAP
5.91); on ve genuine RGB images it reaches 4.884 bpp (GAP 4.787) and
MAE 6.56 (GAP 8.03). The advantage is not uniform: GAP is better on 1
of 6 grayscale images and on 5 of 15 RGB channels. An error-distribution
analysis shows why. The error-to-capacity mapping is a non-monotone stair-
case with a 7-bit reward for exact prediction, so a predictor with lower MAE
but fewer exactly correct pixels can carry less payload. Exact reversibility
is veried by direct array comparison in 267 end-to-end runs with payloads
from zero bits to full capacity (179 with the reference implementation and 88
with a separately validated vectorized implementation), and recovery unique-
ness is checked exhaustively over all 256 × 256 (pixel, prediction) pairs. We
derive closed-form parameter and multiplyaccumulate counts and measure

1. Introduction
Reversible data hiding (RDH) embeds auxiliary data into a cover image
such that both the data and the original cover can be restored without error
[1, 2]. Exact restoration distinguishes RDH from conventional watermarking
and makes it relevant to medical, forensic, military and archival imaging,
where even a single modied pixel can be unacceptable. When the cover must
also remain condential, for example when images are stored on an untrusted
cloud server that must annotate them without seeing them, the problem
becomes reversible data hiding in encrypted images (RDHEI) [3, 4, 5]. An
authorised receiver must be able to decrypt the image, extract the payload
and recover the cover bit for bit, while parties without the key learn nothing
about the image content.
Encryption removes most of the spatial redundancy that plaintext RDH
relies on. The literature has responded in two ways: vacating room after
encryption (VRAE) [4, 6, 5, 7] or reserving room before encryption (RRBE)
[8]. In the RRBE family and in most recent high-capacity schemes [9, 10,
11, 12, 13], redundancy is measured in the plaintext through pixel prediction:
the more concentrated the prediction errors, the more bits each pixel can
carry while preserving a unique inverse. The predictor is therefore not a
preprocessing detail but a rst-order determinant of capacity [14, 15].
Rule-based predictors such as MED [16], GAP and CALIC [17] are cheap
and well understood, but their rules are xed. CNN predictors learn the
mapping from neighbourhood to pixel value from data and have reduced
prediction error in plaintext RDH [18, 19, 20] and in RDHEI [21, 22]. What
is reported less often is the complete accounting a practitioner needs: how
much capacity a learned predictor adds when the embedding rule is held xed;

why it sometimes adds none; whether the pipeline remains exactly reversible
for arbitrary payload lengths; how architectural choices change the outcome;
and what inference costs, both asymptotically and on commodity hardware.
This paper provides that accounting for a compact CNN predictor in-
side a label-map MSB-replacement RDHEI pipeline. Its contributions are as
follows.
1. Controlled predictor comparison. The embedding and recovery
algebra is held xed and only the predictor changes, so capacity dif-
ferences between CNN-3 ×3, MED, GAP and CALIC are attributable
to prediction alone. Results are reported per image and per colour
channel, including every case in which the CNN does not win.
2. Error-distribution analysis. We characterise the error-to-capacity
mapping exactly and use measured error statistics to explain both the
CNN's gains and its losses, showing that mean absolute error is an
imperfect proxy for capacity under MSB-replacement embedding.
3. Architecture ablation. Five trained checkpoints isolate the eect of
patch size, the residual projection and the training loss on capacity,
error, parameter count and runtime.
4. Veried exact reversibility. Reversibility is checked by direct array
comparison, independently of the pipeline's internal ags, over 267 end-
to-end runs that include empty, one-bit, partial, near-maximum and
maximum payloads, and a uniqueness property of the recovery rule is
veried exhaustively over its nite domain.
5. Complexity and cost accounting. We give closed-form parameter
and multiplyaccumulate (MAC) counts, asymptotic costs for every
stage, and predictor-only and end-to-end runtimes under a documented
protocol, together with a vectorized implementation whose outputs are
bitwise identical to the reference.
The contribution is the controlled comparison, the error-distribution ex-
planation, the ablation and the cost analysis; we do not propose a new CNN
architecture, a new embedding rule or a new cryptographic primitive, and we
do not claim that learned prediction should replace rule-based prediction in
general. The aim is to quantify, under controlled conditions, what a compact
learned predictor buys, where it fails, and what it costs. Section 2 reviews
related work, Section 3 describes the scheme and Section 4 the experimental
protocol. Section 5 presents and analyses the results, Section 6 discusses
security and limitations, and Section 7 concludes.

2. Related work
This section reviews plaintext RDH, the two main RDHEI frameworks,
recent label-map and multi-MSB schemes, rule-based and learned pixel pre-
dictors, and evaluation practice. Table 1 summarises representative RDHEI
schemes.
2.1. Reversible data hiding in plaintext images
Early RDH schemes exploited either inter-pixel correlation or the shape
of a statistical distribution. Tian's dierence expansion (DE) [1] transforms
each pixel pair into its integer average and dierence, doubles the dierence
and places one payload bit in the vacated least signicant bit; a location
map records the pairs that would overow and is compressed and embedded
with the payload. DE achieves close to 0.5 bpp per pass but its distortion
grows quickly because every expandable dierence is doubled regardless of
magnitude.
Ni et al. introduced histogram shifting (HS) [2]. The scheme selects a
peak bin of the grey-level histogram and an empty or near-empty zero bin,
shifts the bins between them by one to create a gap next to the peak, and
embeds one bit in every pixel whose value equals the peak. Distortion is
bounded by one grey level per pixel and PSNR stays above about 48 dB, but
capacity is limited to the height of the peak bin, which is small for natural
images.
Prediction-error expansion (PEE) combines the two ideas. Each pixel is
predicted from its neighbours and the prediction-error histogram, rather than
the grey-level histogram, is expanded or shifted. Because prediction errors of
natural images follow a sharply peaked, approximately Laplacian distribution
centred at zero, the peak bins are far higher than in a grey-level histogram,
and capacity and distortion improve together. Gui et al. generalised PEE
so that more than one bit can be embedded into small errors and used a
local complexity measure to direct the payload adaptively to smooth regions,
where errors are small [14]. Wang et al. made both the predictor and the
choice of embedding bins adaptive [15], and Yu et al. [23] combined pairwise
PEE with a decomposition of the two-dimensional prediction-error histogram
(2D-PEH).
The lesson of this line of work, and the premise of the present study,
is that prediction and embedding are coupled. A better predictor does not
change the embedding rule, but it changes how many pixels fall into the
4

cheapest error bins and therefore how much payload the rule can carry at a
given distortion.
2.2. Vacating room after encryption
In VRAE schemes, the content owner encrypts the original image and
a data hider, who knows only the ciphertext, makes room for the payload.
Puech et al. [3] encrypted 4 × 4 blocks with AES and embedded one bit
per block; the receiver restores each block by comparing the local standard
deviation of the candidate decryptions, exploiting the fact that a wrongly
decrypted block looks like noise. Zhang [4] divided a stream-cipher-encrypted
image into blocks, split each block into two pixel sets and ipped the three
least signicant bits of one set according to the payload bit. After decryption,
the receiver recovers the bit and the block by choosing the hypothesis with the
smaller spatial uctuation. Hong et al. [6] improved the uctuation function
so that more pixels contribute to it and used side-match ordering, which lets
already-recovered neighbouring blocks guide later decisions; Liao and Shu [24]
further rened the uctuation measure using the absolute mean dierence of
multiple neighbouring pixels. All of these schemes couple extraction with
decryption and have a non-zero extraction error rate for small blocks.
Zhang's separable scheme [5] removed this coupling. The data hider com-
presses the least signicant bits of the encrypted image with a data-hiding
key and places the payload in the space saved. A receiver with only the data-
hiding key can extract the payload, a receiver with only the encryption key
can decrypt an image that closely resembles the original, and a receiver with
both keys can recover the cover exactly when the payload is small. Qian and
Zhang [7] compressed selected encrypted bits with distributed source coding
based on low-density parity-check codes, using the unencrypted neighbours
available after decryption as side information at the decoder. VRAE has
the advantage that the content owner does no preprocessing, but because
encrypted bits have nearly maximal entropy, the room that can be vacated
is small, typically well below 0.5 bpp.
2.3. Reserving room before encryption and prediction-based RDHEI
Ma et al. [8] reversed the order of operations. Before encryption, the con-
tent owner uses a conventional plaintext RDH algorithm to embed the least
signicant bits of some pixels into other pixels, which reserves room that the
data hider later lls by simple substitution after encryption. Because redun-
dancy is exploited in the plaintext, where it is available, the achievable rate
5

PAGE 7
rises sharply and extraction becomes error free. The price is preprocessing
at the content owner, which is acceptable in most applications because the
owner already has to encrypt the image.
Puteaux and Puech [9] observed that the most signicant bit of a pixel
can usually be predicted from its neighbours. They detect, before encryp-
tion, the pixels whose MSB would be mispredicted, record them in a loca-
tion map, and allow the data hider to replace the MSB of every other pixel
with a payload bit. The receiver decrypts and restores each MSB by predic-
tion. This shifted the focus of RDHEI from the least to the most signicant
bit-planes and raised rates to around 1 bpp. Yin et al. [10] combined pixel
prediction with bit-plane rearrangement and compression: prediction errors
are computed in the plaintext, their bit-planes are rearranged so that the
high-order planes are highly compressible, the compressed planes and auxil-
iary information are encrypted, and the vacated space is oered to the data
hider. Such schemes are self-contained, in that the receiver regenerates the
predictions from already-recovered pixels and only compressed auxiliary in-
formation travels with the image, so their reported rates are net rates.
2.4. Label maps and multi-MSB embedding
Generalising the single-MSB idea, a large group of recent schemes assigns
each pixel a label that states how many of its high-order bits can be predicted
from the prediction value, and replaces all of those bits with payload. The
capacity of the image is the sum of the labels, so it is set directly by the
distribution of prediction errors, and the label map itself must be compressed
and conveyed to the receiver. Research in this group diers mainly in how
the prediction is formed and how the label map is coded.
Ren et al. combined multiple predictions with adaptive Human encoding
[25], and later with an ecient parametric binary tree labelling [13]. Qi et
al. [11] and Yue et al. [12] combined adaptive quadtree partitioning of the
image with MSB prediction. Ankur et al. [26] encoded the label map bit-
plane by bit-plane with an optimal block size. Zhang et al. [27] combined
prediction-error modication with basic block compression, and Huang et
al. [28] used bidirectional encoding to enhance the payload. Qiu [29] and
Qiu et al. [30] used edge-directed prediction, combined with multi-MSB self-
prediction and with adaptive entropy coding respectively. Kim et al. [31]
proposed a pixel shifting approach (PSA) for RDHEI, and Ren et al. [32]
combined reversible data hiding with authentication of the encrypted image
on the basis of prediction-error compression.

Two observations from this group motivate the present study. First, much
of the work concentrates on how the label map is coded and how the image
is partitioned, so the predictor is a design variable that can be studied sep-
arately. Second, because capacity is a sum of per-pixel labels, the quantity
that matters is not the mean absolute error but the full distribution of er-
rors as seen through the label rule. We make this second point explicit in
Section 5.3.
2.5. Rule-based pixel predictors
The median edge detector (MED) of LOCO-I/JPEG-LS [16] predicts a
pixel from its west, north and north-west neighbours. If the north-west neigh-
bour is larger than both others it predicts their minimum, if smaller it pre-
dicts their maximum, and otherwise it predicts the planar value W +N −N W;
this simple rule detects horizontal and vertical edges at a cost of three reads
and two comparisons per pixel. The gradient-adjusted predictor (GAP) of
CALIC [17] estimates the horizontal and vertical gradients from seven causal
neighbours and selects among sharp-edge, weak-edge and smooth predictions
using xed thresholds on the gradient dierence. CALIC additionally re-
nes the GAP value with context-based error feedback. These predictors are
causal, deterministic and extremely fast, which is why they dominate RDH
and RDHEI practice, but their rules and thresholds were designed for lossless
compression and are not adapted to any particular image class.
2.6. Learned predictors for RDH and RDHEI
Hu and Xiang [18] combined CNN-based pixel prediction with adaptive
embedding. Yang and Huang [19] proposed a new CNN-based predictor
for RDH. Bae et al. [20] studied a CNN-based scheme through the relation
between PSNR and embedding capacity. In the encrypted domain, Ping et al.
[21] introduced asymmetric CNN and adaptive-mean predictors for RDHEI,
and Cheng et al. [22] combined a pixel-level masked autoencoder with polar
codes.
These studies indicate that learned predictors can reduce prediction error
relative to rule-based ones. The present study focuses on three questions:
whether a capacity gain survives when the embedding rule is held xed and
only the predictor changes; whether larger receptive elds and larger models
help for masked single-pixel prediction; and what a learned predictor costs
at inference, in operations and in wall-clock time, relative to rule-based pre-
dictors.

2.7. Evaluation practice
Reversible schemes are judged by capacity, usually in bits per pixel, by the
visual quality of the marked or directly decrypted image, usually by PSNR
and SSIM [33, 34], and by exactness of recovery. For a scheme that restores
the cover exactly, PSNR between cover and recovered image is innite and
SSIM is one, so these gures conrm reversibility but do not distinguish
between schemes. Most papers report results on a small set of standard test
images from the USC-SIPI database [35], such as Lena, Airplane, Baboon and
Peppers, and many also report averages over the BOSSbase [36] or BOWS-2
collections. In RDHEI, it is also customary to distinguish the gross number of
bits that the labels allow from the net payload that remains after the auxiliary
information has been accounted for; only the latter can be compared across
schemes.
2.8. Positioning of this work
The present work lies at the intersection of Sections 2.4 and 2.6. It takes
the label-map MSB-replacement mechanism as xed and asks how much a
compact learned predictor changes the label distribution, why, and at what
cost. It does not propose a new label-map coder, and its rates are gross rather
than net (Section 3.7); consequently, it compares predictors with one another
rather than with the published net rates of complete RDHEI schemes.
3. Proposed scheme
3.1. Overview
Fig. 1 summarises the pipeline. The content owner (i) predicts every
pixel, (ii) converts each absolute prediction error into a per-pixel label giving
the number n ∈ {0, . . . ,7} of replaceable MSBs and a recovery band, (iii)
replaces those MSBs with payload bits, and (iv) encrypts the marked image
with AES-CTR [37, 38]. The receiver decrypts, reads the payload from the
MSBs, and restores each pixel by a bounded candidate search. Embedding
therefore happens in the plaintext, before encryption, by the content owner;
the scheme belongs to the RRBE family [8], with the owner and the data hider
played by the same party. Only the predictor diers between the compared
methods.

3.4. Embedding by MSB replacement
Pixels are visited in raster order. For a pixel with n > 0, the next n
payload bits (MSB rst) form q ∈ [0, 2n − 1], and
p′ = q · 28−n +  p mod 28−n , (7)
so the lower 8−n bits are preserved. Pixels with n = 0 are unchanged. Every

3.5. Encryption
The marked image is encrypted with AES-CTR using a 256-bit key and
a 128-bit counter initialised from a nonce. For RGB images each channel
is encrypted with a distinct nonce N (c)c = N0 + c + 1, c ∈ {0, 1, 2}, so no
keystream segment is reused across channels.
3.6. Extraction and recovery
The receiver decrypts with the same key and nonce(s), visits pixels in the
same raster order, and reads the top n bits of each marked pixel until the
declared payload length has been collected. Recovery then continues over
all pixels regardless of payload length. For n >0, let l = p′ mod 28−n. The
candidate set is
C(p′, n) = {a · 28−n + l : a = 0, . . . ,2n − 1}, (8)
and the recovered value is the candidate c satisfying mmin ≤ |c − ˆp| ≤mmax.
Proposition 1 (Unique recovery) . For every pair (p, ˆp) ∈ {0, . . . ,255}2 with

3.7. Side information and separability
Recovery requires the key, nonce(s), payload length and the owner-
computed maps b, mmin, mmax and ˆp. Because the CNN uses a non-causal
neighbourhood of the original image, the receiver cannot regenerate ˆp from
the marked image, so the maps must be delivered out of band. The scheme
is therefore separable (decryption and extraction are distinct steps with
distinct inputs) but not blind. The capacities reported in Section 5 are gross
rates that do not deduct this side information. They compare predictors
fairly with one another, because all predictors are charged identically, but
they are not directly comparable with the net rates of self-contained schemes
such as [9, 10, 13]. Section 6 returns to this point.
4. Experimental setup
Test images. The grayscale set consists of six les identied by name as
the standard benchmark images Airplane, Baboon, Goldhill, Jetplane, Lena
and Peppers. The colour set consists of ve les named 4.2.03, 4.2.05,

and a colour Lena image. Before the experiments, every le was decoded
and checked for resolution and channel mode: the grayscale les are single-
channel, 512 × 512 except Goldhill ( 720 × 576) and Jetplane ( 1024 × 1024);
the colour les are 512 × 512 × 3 with channels that are not all identical.
A sixth candidate colour le that decoded as a single channel was excluded
from all RGB statistics. All eleven les were included in every reported ex-
periment. Their origin was not veried against the original distributions, so
the identication is by name and content type only. Further images were
examined during development but are not reported; the per-image analysis
is restricted to this common reference set so that the tables can be read
alongside published ones. Together the sets cover smooth (Airplane, 4.2.05),
textured (Baboon, 4.2.03) and mixed content.
Predictors. MED [16], GAP and CALIC [17] were implemented with causal

MED uses edge padding so that the rst row and column also receive real
predictions; this variant gives slightly higher capacity (4.978 vs. 4.958 bpp

CNN checkpoints are listed in Table 9.

5.3. Prediction-error analysis
By Eq. (6), capacity depends on the error distribution only through the
label staircase of Table 4. Reducing MAE helps only to the extent that it
moves probability mass into higher steps, and the steps are uneven. The
measured error statistics expose three mechanisms.
Exact prediction. Because ϵ = 0 carries 7 bits and ϵ = 1 carries 6, the fraction
of pixels predicted exactly, π7, has an outsized eect. On 4.2.07 B, CNN-3 ×3
has the lower MAE (5.300 vs. 5.826 for GAP) but the lower capacity (4.940
vs. 4.971 bpp): GAP predicts 9.93% of pixels exactly, the CNN only 6.09%.
The CNN's errors are tighter overall but are rarely exactly zero, and the same
pattern explains its losses on 4.2.06 R and G. A rounded regression output
from a network trained with an L1-type loss has no particular reason to land
exactly on the integer target, whereas a rule-based predictor reproduces it
exactly whenever the local structure matches its rule.
Mid-range compression and tail removal. Pooled over the 15 RGB channels,
GAP and the CNN predict the same fraction of pixels exactly, 8.68%. The
CNN's aggregate gain therefore comes from elsewhere: it converts GAP's

5.8. Discussion
The results support four conclusions. First, under a xed embedding rule
the learned predictor raises mean capacity by 26% and lowers mean MAE
by 1834% relative to the rule-based predictors, but not uniformly: GAP
remains better on one grayscale image and on 5 of 15 RGB channels, and no
CNN variant studied closes those gaps. Second, the error-distribution anal-
ysis explains the mismatch between MAE and capacity: the label staircase
rewards exact prediction disproportionately, and the CNN wins by compress-
ing mid-range errors and removing the dead tail rather than by predicting
more pixels exactly. This suggests that training objectives aligned with the
staircase are a more promising route to capacity than larger models. Third,
for this masked single-pixel task more context and more parameters hurt
rather than help, so the best CNN is also the smallest. Fourth, the improve-
ment costs two to three orders of magnitude in prediction time and about
8% more expected recovery work. In the reference prototype this is partly
hidden by per-pixel loop overhead; once that overhead is removed, prediction
dominates. Whether the trade-o is worthwhile depends on whether capacity
or throughput is the binding constraint in a given application.
6. Security analysis and limitations
Condentiality. The marked image is encrypted with AES-CTR under a 256-
bit key [37, 38]. Under the standard assumption that AES behaves as a pseu-
dorandom permutation, CTR mode provides condentiality against chosen-
plaintext attacks provided that the key is secret and no counter value is ever
reused under the same key [38]; this is the only security property claimed
for the encryption layer, and no formal security analysis of the complete
system is attempted. Within an image, distinct per-channel nonces prevent
keystream reuse across RGB channels; across images encrypted under the
same key, nonce uniqueness is the caller's responsibility. An earlier version
of our RGB code reused one nonce for all channels, which would have exposed
cR ⊕ cG = I ′R ⊕ I ′G; all results here use the corrected code.
Integrity. AES-CTR is unauthenticated, so the receiver cannot detect cipher-
text tampering, and any modication of the ciphertext propagates bit for bit
into the marked image and breaks exact recovery. Authenticated encryp-
tion (e.g. AES-GCM) or a MAC over the ciphertext and side information
should be added in deployment; integrated authentication schemes such as
[32] address this directly.
30

PAGE 32
Side information and net capacity. As discussed in Section 3.7, recovery
needs the owner's label maps and prediction map, which the receiver can-
not regenerate because the CNN neighbourhood is non-causal. Transmitted
uncompressed, this side information exceeds the payload, so the reported
rates measure predictor quality under a common mapping rather than a de-
ployable net rate. Making the scheme self-contained requires either a causal
(raster-order) predictor that the receiver can rerun on already-recovered pix-
els, so that only a compressed label map must be conveyed, or compression
and embedding of the maps in the reserved room, as in label-map RDHEI
schemes [10, 13, 26]. This is the most important direction for future work.
Scope. No evaluation was performed against malicious senders or receivers,
key leakage, side channels or lossy re-encoding; any lossy operation destroys
exactness by design. Statistical tests of the encrypted images (histograms,
entropy, correlation) were not part of this study. The test set is six grayscale
and ve RGB images, so all comparisons are descriptive. The dead zones of
the label map ( ϵ = 64 and ϵ ≥ 128) are a property of the shared embedding
algebra and were deliberately left unchanged; removing them would consti-
tute a dierent method. Training was performed on a separate machine and
its logs did not survive, so training is not fully reproducible; the evaluated
checkpoints are xed by their digests (Table 3). The test images are iden-
tied by name and checked for size and channel mode, but their origin was
not veried against the original distributions.
7. Conclusion
This paper evaluated a compact CNN pixel predictor within a label-map
MSB-replacement RDHEI pipeline, holding the embedding and recovery al-
gebra xed so that dierences arise from prediction alone. The 46,881-
parameter CNN-3 ×3 achieved the highest mean gross capacity (5.190 bpp
grayscale, 4.884 bpp RGB) and the lowest mean prediction error among MED,
GAP, CALIC and four CNN variants, while GAP remained better on one
grayscale image and ve of fteen RGB channels. An exact characterisation
of the error-to-label staircase, combined with measured error statistics, ex-
plained both outcomes: the CNN gains by compressing mid-range errors and
removing large-error tails, and loses where a rule-based predictor reproduces
more pixels exactly. Larger patches and larger models predicted worse, and
weighting large errors in the loss did not improve capacity. Exact reversibility

held in all 267 valid end-to-end runs of the reference and vectorized imple-
mentations; this is an empirical result over the tested cases, supported by an
exhaustive check of the per-pixel recovery rule, not a proof for all images and
payloads. We derived closed-form parameter and MAC counts and measured
CNN prediction at 325× the cost of MED on a laptop CPU; a vectorized,
output-identical implementation reduced the complete RGB pipeline time by
a third. Future work will (i) design a causal learned predictor so that the
receiver can regenerate predictions and only a compressed label map must
be conveyed, enabling net-rate comparison with state-of-the-art RDHEI; (ii)
train with objectives aligned with the label staircase, including colour-specic
training; (iii) add authenticated encryption; and (iv) extend the evaluation
to larger image collections.
